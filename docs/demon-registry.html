The Affliction Registry

Twenty demons written to replace the fantasy roster. None of them is a god, a devil, or anything anyone has ever prayed to. Every entry's appearance gives away its affinity chart — and this draft orders them so that each tier is answerable with the kit you actually have when you reach it, which the first draft got wrong. The two banishing lines, light and dark, do not wound anything; they take a demon out of the world or they fail.

This draft adds the four demons that are already in the code — three wardens and a boss — which are waiting on nothing but sprites, and brings the notes in line with a spell list where every element is learnable at three different reaches.

Affinity states, and what each costs in press turns

WEAKDouble damage, half an icon — unless the target is guarding, which denies both.
RESISTHalf damage, still a full icon. Wasteful, not fatal.
NULLNo damage, and it costs two icons. Punishing.
REPELThrown back at the caster. Your phase ends on the spot.
DRAINIt heals for the damage. Your phase ends on the spot.

Every element comes at three reaches

There is no spell tier any more, only a spell width. Fire, ice, thunder, light and dark are each learnable from the first floor at three reaches: one demon, two or three at random, or every demon standing. Nothing gets stronger later; things get wider later, and width is the thing you pay for.

Width is charged twice, in MP and again in what reaches each demon: a damage spell keeps 75% of itself across two or three and 60% across a room, and a banishing spell keeps only 70% and 50%, because expelling four demons at once for 36 MP would end the fight rather than shape it. That second charge is what keeps the single-target spell in a loadout of four slots.

This changes how a line of demons should be read, not just one demon. A wide cast takes the worst thing that happened to it — one demon repelling it ends the phase for the whole cast — with a single deliberate exception: a lone NULL among demons that took it is not worth two icons, so only a cast that every demon nulled pays that. A weakness anywhere still pays its half-icon back. So a mixed line is exactly where an ALL spell stops being a straight upgrade, and the charts in this registry are what tell you which line you are looking at.

Light and dark are not two more elements

They do no damage at all. A cast of Banish or Consign either takes the demon out of the fight entirely or does nothing whatsoever, and the chart decides which. Read across those two columns and the words mean something else than they do in the first four: WEAK is not double damage, it is gone roughly three times in five. NULL is not zero damage, it is immune, and you spent two icons finding out.

The odds come off the demon's own chart — 62% into a weakness, 28% against an ordinary demon, 8% into a resistance, and nothing at all through null, repel or drain. One slot spent on Banish is a bet that you will meet the right demon; the payoff is a whole enemy for one icon, which is why it is worth the Fire spell it displaces.

The second half of the alignment line is now load-bearing. It used to be flavour. It is the rule: no Light-aligned demon is ever weak to Banish, and no Dark-aligned demon is ever weak to Consign. Every demon that Consign answers outright is Neutral·Light, without exception. So the alignment tells you, before you have read anything else, which of the two spells to leave at home.

Luck moves the coin flip and never the chart. The detective has a fifth stat now, and on these two columns it is worth spending: it nudges the odds on the rows that were already uncertain, by up to fifteen points either way. It does not touch NULL, REPEL or DRAIN at all — a demon that cannot abide nothing is immune to a lucky detective and an unlucky one alike — and each row keeps its own ceiling, so a RESIST tops out at 18% however well he rolls. Luck buys an edge on a maybe. It never buys a different answer.

Neither line is ever dead weight. Every tier holds at least one demon weak to light and at least one weak to dark, so whichever you equipped, the floor you are standing on has something on it that the spell answers.

Why the order is the design

A tier is only balanced if it is balanced against what the player is holding. The first draft of this roster had an even spread overall — four phys, six fire, five ice, five thunder — and read as balanced. Tallied per tier it was not: floor one had zero demons weak to a blade, one weak to Fire, and contained both a fire drain and a phys null. The detective starts with a blade and Ember, the single-target fire spell. The first floor punished both of the only two things he owns.

So the punishing states are staged rather than sprinkled. Tier I has none at all. Tier II introduces drains. Tier III introduces nulls. Tier IV is where things throw your own attack back at you. Each tier stays answerable with the kit you plausibly have by the time you reach it.

The banishing lines were staged on the same clock, one step later. The detective cannot reliably hold Banish or Consign until an orb on the second floor sells him one, so tiers I and II carry no null, drain or repel on either column at all — only a single weakness each way, which is a reward for having found a scroll early rather than a demand that you did. The first banishing nulls arrive in tier III alongside the elemental ones. The drain and the repel wait for tier IV, where a player who has spent the whole run leaning on one of these spells finally meets the forge that swallows it and the glass that sends it back.

Alignment still tells you which Talk option lands, one-to-one with no exceptions: Law answers to Logic, Chaos to Gain, Neutral·Light to Flatter, Neutral·Dark to Survival.

Talk difficulty still stops at 3. At 4 a single round can never gain more than one point against a threshold of four, so recruitment becomes arithmetically impossible. Three demons refuse outright instead, which is honest where an unreachable check is not.
